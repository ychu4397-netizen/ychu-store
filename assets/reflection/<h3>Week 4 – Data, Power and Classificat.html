Week 4 – Data, Power and Classification

The Week 3 web-scraping workshop helped me realise that collecting data is already a process of selection. A scraper can gather many records quickly, but the results depend on what the website makes accessible and what we choose to collect. We also have to decide how to organise and classify the information.

D’Ignazio and Klein’s reading made me question categories that may seem natural or neutral. What gets counted can become visible and influential, while people or experiences that do not fit the categories may be overlooked. Crawford’s discussion also reminded me that data used in digital systems is not simply a resource without a history; its collection is connected to wider structures of power and labour.

As a regular YouTube user, I am comfortable with some data being used to make search and recommendations more useful, but I do not fully understand how my data is used. This makes me think about the difference between accepting a service and knowing what happens to the information I provide or generate through using it.

One challenge of web scraping is that collected information does not explain itself. We need to consider what is missing, how our categories shape the results and whether collecting or sharing the data could affect people’s privacy. I would avoid including usernames or other details that could identify individuals in screenshots.

Readings: D’Ignazio, C. and Klein, L. F. (2020). “What Gets Counted Counts.” In Data Feminism, pp. 97–123. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.

Crawford, K. (2021). “Data.” In Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence, pp. 89–122. New Haven: Yale University Press.
