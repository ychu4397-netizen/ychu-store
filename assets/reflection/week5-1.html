Week 5 – Data Visualisation

Feigenbaum and Alamalhodaei’s reading helped me understand that a visualisation is not a neutral display of facts. Choices about the data, categories, design and story all affect what viewers notice and how they interpret the information.

Reflecting on the Week 4 workshop, I learned that developing a data story involves more than collecting information. We need to decide what question we are asking, which data is relevant and how to classify it. The same data may suggest different insights when collected or organised for different purposes.

I also became more aware of ethical questions. Data can leave out people or experiences, and categories may reproduce bias if we treat them as natural or complete. We should protect privacy by removing identifying details, such as social media usernames, from any screenshots we share.

One challenge in visualising data is making a chart clear and engaging without oversimplifying the results. I learned that the design process is part of the analysis: it shapes the story the data can tell, as well as what remains unseen.

Reading: Feigenbaum, A. and Alamalhodaei, A. (2020). “Visual Data Storytelling.” In The Data Storytelling Workbook. Routledge.
