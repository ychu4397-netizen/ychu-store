Week 3 – Web Scraping: Turning Web Pages into Research Data

This week introduced web scraping as a method for collecting data from online sources. Before this topic, it was easy to think of a website mainly as something people read, watch or interact with. Web scraping encourages me to look at websites differently: their content is organised through underlying structures, and some of these structures can be identified and collected for research.

An important idea I took from this topic is that online information does not exist independently of its presentation. Web pages use elements such as headings, links, lists and other HTML components to organise content. When researchers target particular elements, they can extract specific information from a larger page. This means that understanding the structure of a website is important not only for building digital artefacts, but also for studying them as sources of data.

The reading by Venturini and Rogers (2025) provides a useful framework for thinking about scraping documents and interfaces as a digital method. Scraping is not simply a technical process of copying information. Researchers must decide what to collect, which parts of a page are relevant, and how to transform the collected material into a dataset. These decisions influence what becomes available for analysis and what may be left out.

One challenge of web scraping is that the structure of a website may change. A selector that identifies a particular element may stop working if the page layout or HTML changes. Some information may also be loaded dynamically, making it difficult to collect using a simple scraping approach. In addition, scraped data may contain missing values, irrelevant material or inconsistent formats. The resulting dataset therefore needs to be checked rather than treated as a complete and accurate representation of the original website.

I also think it is important to distinguish between information that is technically accessible and information that is appropriate to collect. Public visibility does not automatically mean that every use of the information is ethically unproblematic. Researchers should consider the website's terms and conditions, the sensitivity of the information, the expectations of the people who produced it, and the potential consequences of collecting and republishing it.

Questions of anonymity and consent are particularly important when online material relates to identifiable people. Even when names are removed, a combination of quotations or contextual details may make someone recognisable. This suggests that ethical decisions should be considered throughout the research process, from selecting a source and collecting data to storing, analysing and presenting the results.

This topic also connects to my portfolio website. The website itself has a structure that determines how my work is organised and presented. The titles, images and expandable sections make some information easier to find than other information. Thinking about websites as structured data helps me recognise that the organisation of digital content affects what users can see, collect and interpret.

Overall, web scraping has encouraged me to think more critically about how online research data is produced. A dataset collected from a website is not simply the website transferred into another format. It is the result of technical choices, selection rules and ethical decisions. For research to be meaningful, I need to consider not only what the data contains, but also how it was collected, what might be missing, and what limitations this creates for the conclusions I can draw.

Reading: Venturini, T. and Rogers, R. (2025). Chapter 6, “Scraping Documents and Interfaces.” In Digital Methods: A Short Introduction. Cambridge: Polity Press.

Further reading: Tiidenberg, K. (2018). “Ethics in Digital Research.” In U. Flick (ed.), The SAGE Handbook of Qualitative Data Collection, pp. 466–479. London: SAGE Publications Ltd.
